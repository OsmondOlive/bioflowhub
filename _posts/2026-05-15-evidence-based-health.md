We live in an era of unprecedented access to health information, and unprecedented confusion about what to do with it. Wellness influencers, supplement companies, news headlines, and well-meaning friends all confidently contradict each other, leaving most people uncertain about even basic questions.

The solution is not to find better sources of authority to trust blindly. It is to develop a working understanding of how health evidence is generated, evaluated, and misrepresented, so you can assess claims yourself.

This post covers the fundamentals of evidence-based thinking applied to health.

---

## The Hierarchy of Evidence

Not all health research is equally reliable. The scientific community uses a hierarchy of evidence to assess the strength of different types of studies.

**Anecdote and case reports** occupy the bottom. One person's experience, even a dramatic one, tells us very little about what will happen in other people. Human bodies are variable, placebo effects are real, and our memories of cause and effect are unreliable.

**Observational studies** observe patterns in groups of people without intervening. They can identify associations but cannot establish causation. "People who eat more vegetables live longer" is an association. It does not prove that vegetables cause longevity, because vegetable eaters differ from non-vegetable eaters in dozens of other ways.

**Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)** are the gold standard for testing whether a specific intervention causes a specific outcome. Participants are randomly assigned to receive the intervention or a control condition, which controls for confounding variables. Well-conducted RCTs are the most reliable evidence for causation.

**Systematic reviews and meta-analyses** pool data from multiple studies to produce more statistically powerful estimates. A well-conducted meta-analysis of multiple high-quality RCTs represents the strongest available evidence for any health claim.

When evaluating a health claim, the first question should be: what type of study is this based on?

## The Difference Between Association and Causation

This is the single most important distinction in health science literacy, and the one most frequently obscured in media coverage.

"Eating breakfast is associated with lower body weight" does not mean eating breakfast causes weight loss. People who eat breakfast regularly may differ from those who skip it in dozens of other ways: sleep habits, stress levels, overall dietary patterns, physical activity. Any of these might explain the association.

Headlines routinely present associations as causation. "Coffee linked to longer life," "Red meat linked to cancer," "Screen time linked to depression" are all associations, not necessarily causal relationships. Sometimes the causation is real. Sometimes the association disappears when confounders are properly controlled. Sometimes causation runs in the reverse direction.

Establishing causation requires either a randomized experiment or a theoretical mechanism combined with multiple converging lines of evidence from different research groups using different methods.

## Publication Bias and Reproducibility

The scientific literature has a systematic problem: studies with positive or statistically significant results are more likely to be published than studies with null results. This means the published literature overrepresents positive findings, creating a misleadingly optimistic picture of many interventions.

This is partly why many findings that generate significant excitement, whether for a particular supplement, dietary pattern, or pharmaceutical drug, fail to replicate when independent researchers attempt to reproduce them.

A landmark project called the Reproducibility Project attempted to replicate 100 studies published in top psychology journals and found that approximately 36% produced results consistent with the original findings. In medicine and nutrition, the picture is similar.

This does not mean science is unreliable. It means that single studies, especially novel or surprising ones, should be treated with significant skepticism until replicated across multiple independent research groups.

## Industry Funding and Conflicts of Interest

Research funded by industries with a financial stake in the outcome consistently produces more favorable findings for those industries than independently funded research. This pattern appears across pharmaceuticals, nutrition science, and beyond.

This does not mean industry-funded research is automatically wrong. It means it should be evaluated with heightened scrutiny, particularly when it contradicts the weight of independent evidence.

A well-known historical example is the sugar industry's funding of research in the 1960s that shifted attention away from sugar and toward dietary fat as a driver of heart disease, a manipulation that influenced public health recommendations for decades.

When evaluating health claims, always check: who funded the study? Do the researchers have financial relationships with companies that benefit from the findings? Is this finding replicated in independently funded research?

## How to Evaluate Health Claims Practically

A few questions worth asking before acting on any health claim.

**What type of evidence is this?** Anecdote, observational study, RCT, or meta-analysis? Each carries a different level of confidence.

**What was actually measured?** Many studies measure surrogate endpoints, biomarkers assumed to correlate with outcomes, rather than the outcomes themselves. A supplement that improves a biomarker does not necessarily improve the health outcome the biomarker represents.

**How large was the effect?** A statistically significant result can be biologically trivial. An intervention that reduces relative risk by 30% sounds impressive. If the baseline risk is 0.1%, the absolute risk reduction is 0.03%.

**Has it been replicated?** A single study, even a well-conducted one, is preliminary evidence. Convergence across multiple independent studies is what builds genuine confidence.

**Is there a plausible mechanism?** Biologically implausible claims, regardless of the study design, deserve extra scrutiny.

**Who benefits from you believing this?** Not a reason to dismiss a claim, but a reason to apply additional critical scrutiny.

## The Limits of Individual Optimization

A final note that I think is important from a biomedical perspective: health is probabilistic, not deterministic. You can do everything right, eat well, exercise regularly, sleep sufficiently, manage stress, and still experience illness. And you can do many things imperfectly and live a long, healthy life.

The goal of evidence-based health behavior is to move probabilities in favorable directions, not to guarantee outcomes. This is both humbling and liberating. It means taking reasonable, evidence-backed actions without becoming paralyzed by every new finding or obsessive about achieving perfection.

Consistency in the fundamentals, the things that appear across all the strongest evidence, matters far more than optimizing the margins.
