Most people receive their lab results as a list of numbers, each next to a reference range, with little explanation of what those numbers mean in practice. Some fall neatly within range and are dismissed. Others trigger anxiety or confusion without enough context to act on them meaningfully.

As a biomedical scientist with published research in cellular biology, I have spent years studying the mechanisms behind these markers. This post is a practical guide to the tests that matter most, what they actually measure, and how to interpret them beyond the binary of normal and abnormal.

---

## A Note on Reference Ranges

Reference ranges are derived from population statistics, not from what is optimal for health. A result that falls within the "normal" range simply means it falls within the range observed in the middle 95% of a reference population. It does not mean the result is optimal.

This distinction matters. For many markers, the difference between "within range" and "optimized" is clinically meaningful. There is real value in understanding where you fall within a range, not just whether you are inside or outside it.

## Complete Blood Count (CBC)

The CBC is one of the most commonly ordered tests. It measures the cellular components of blood and provides information about several systems simultaneously.

**Red blood cells and hemoglobin** measure the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity. Low hemoglobin indicates anemia, which can result from iron deficiency, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, chronic disease, or blood loss. High hemoglobin can indicate dehydration or less common conditions.

**White blood cells** reflect immune system activity. Elevated counts suggest infection, inflammation, or hematological conditions. Chronically low counts may indicate immunosuppression.

**Neutrophils** are the most abundant white cell type and the first responders to bacterial infection. As a researcher who has published on neutrophil biology, I can say that the neutrophil nucleus itself plays a fascinating role in how these cells function and migrate to sites of infection.

**Platelets** are essential for blood clotting. Very low platelets increase bleeding risk; very high counts can increase clotting risk.

## Metabolic Panel: Blood Glucose and Insulin

**Fasting glucose** measures blood sugar after an overnight fast. The conventional reference range is below 100 mg/dL, with pre-diabetes defined as 100 to 125 mg/dL and diabetes at or above 126 mg/dL on two separate occasions.

From a preventive standpoint, tracking trends in fasting glucose over time is more valuable than any single measurement. A result of 95 mg/dL that has risen from 78 mg/dL over three years tells a different story than a stable 95 mg/dL.

**HbA1c** (glycated hemoglobin) reflects average blood glucose over the preceding 2 to 3 months, making it less susceptible to day-to-day variation than fasting glucose. It is a more reliable long-term marker of glucose regulation.

**Fasting insulin** is not routinely ordered but provides valuable information about insulin sensitivity that glucose alone does not capture. Normal fasting glucose with elevated fasting insulin suggests insulin resistance, where the body is compensating but the underlying mechanism is already impaired.

- American Diabetes Association. (2023). Standards of Medical Care in Diabetes. *Diabetes Care*, 46(Suppl 1). [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36507635/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}

## Lipid Panel

The standard lipid panel measures total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides. Each tells a different part of the story.

**LDL cholesterol** is often called "bad cholesterol," but the picture is more nuanced. LDL particle size and number, measured by advanced lipid testing, are more predictive of cardiovascular risk than total LDL concentration alone.

**HDL cholesterol** is involved in reverse cholesterol transport, moving cholesterol from tissues back to the liver. Higher HDL is generally protective, but very high HDL in the absence of healthy lifestyle behaviors may not confer the expected benefit.

**Triglycerides** reflect dietary fat and carbohydrate intake, as well as metabolic health. Elevated triglycerides, particularly above 150 mg/dL, are associated with insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and cardiovascular risk. They respond rapidly to dietary changes, making them a useful short-term marker of metabolic improvement.

**The triglyceride-to-HDL ratio** (triglycerides divided by HDL) is a useful surrogate marker for insulin resistance. A ratio below 2 is generally favorable; above 3 suggests metabolic dysfunction.

## Liver Function Tests

**ALT and AST** are enzymes released when liver cells are damaged. Elevated levels indicate hepatocellular injury from causes including fatty liver disease, alcohol, medications, or viral hepatitis.

Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease is increasingly prevalent and strongly associated with insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome. It can progress silently for years, making routine liver enzyme monitoring important even in the absence of symptoms.

## Thyroid Function

**TSH** is the primary screening test for thyroid dysfunction. It rises when the thyroid is underactive and falls when it is overactive.

Hypothyroidism is significantly more common in women and frequently presents with fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, and cognitive slowing, symptoms that are easy to attribute to other causes. Many cases go undiagnosed for years.

**Free T4 and free T3** provide additional information about thyroid hormone levels when TSH is borderline or symptoms persist despite a normal TSH.

## Vitamin D

Vitamin D deficiency is extraordinarily common, affecting an estimated 1 billion people globally. Despite this, it remains undertested in many routine health evaluations.

The active form of vitamin D functions more like a hormone than a vitamin, with receptors present on virtually every cell in the body. It regulates calcium metabolism, immune function, gene expression, and inflammatory pathways.

The 25-hydroxyvitamin D test is the appropriate screening test. Most researchers now consider levels below 30 ng/mL to be insufficient, with optimal function more consistently associated with levels of 40 to 60 ng/mL.

**References:**
- Holick MF. (2007). Vitamin D deficiency. *New England Journal of Medicine*, 357(3), 266-281. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17634462/){:target="_blank" rel="noopener"}

## Inflammatory Markers

**High-sensitivity CRP** can detect low levels of chronic inflammation associated with cardiovascular risk, even in the absence of acute illness.

An hs-CRP below 1 mg/L is considered low risk; 1 to 3 mg/L is intermediate; above 3 mg/L is high risk for cardiovascular events. Because CRP rises with any acute illness or injury, it should be interpreted in the context of your overall health status, not as an isolated number.

## How to Get the Most From Your Lab Results

Request a copy of your results every time. You have a right to your own medical data, and tracking trends over time is far more informative than any single measurement.

Ask your doctor not just whether your results are "normal" but where they fall within the range, and whether the trend over time is moving in a favorable or unfavorable direction.

If you have results you do not understand, do not simply accept a dismissal. Ask for an explanation. Understanding your own biology is not a privilege. It is information you are entitled to use in making decisions about your health.
